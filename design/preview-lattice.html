Guillermo Fandos

How far animals move, and why individuals of the same species move so differently.

I study the processes that set where animal populations are and how many there are, combining fieldwork, continental-scale data synthesis and Bayesian hierarchical modelling. Assistant Professor at the Universidad Complutense de Madrid.

Fig. 1

Simulated natal dispersal from a single ringing site: 90 trajectories with log-normally distributed distances. Most individuals settle within a few kilometres; a handful, marked in ochre, travel an order of magnitude farther. That heavy tail is the point — it is what makes dispersal hard to estimate and easy to underestimate. Schematic, not fitted to data.

Research

Three questions, one problem: linking individual movement to where species end up

DataEURING ring recoveries
National ringing schemes

Animal movement in dynamic landscapes

Daily foraging, seasonal migration and once-in-a-lifetime dispersal events all leave a signature in ring-recovery data. I use that signature to estimate dispersal kernels and ask what they mean for connectivity and range boundaries.

DataeBird citizen science
Camera traps · AudioMoth

Improving biodiversity monitoring and modelling

Distribution models that ignore dispersal, population dynamics and imperfect detection give confident answers to the wrong question. I build models that carry those processes explicitly and integrate data sources that were never designed to be combined.

DataRange and threat databases
Landscape permeability layers

Conservation under global change

Which species are most exposed, why, and where the problems will surface first — from Iberian large carnivore recolonisation to mammal declines in the Gran Chaco.

INTRADISP

Why individuals of one species disperse differently

Synthesising intraspecific variation in dispersal across European birds, to see how much of the variation we usually average away actually matters for range forecasts.

RIMed-Fauna

Wildlife in rivers that run dry

Mediterranean rivers flood in winter and disappear in summer. Camera trapping and distribution modelling in the National Parks network to see how terrestrial vertebrates track that swing.
